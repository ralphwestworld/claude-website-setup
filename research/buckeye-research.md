## What the genre's biggest winners actually do

The category is dominated by a handful of firms with a remarkably
consistent commercial structure. We're modeling on the *pattern*, not
copying any one spot.

### Morgan & Morgan ("For The People")

- **TV spend:** ~$240M/yr in 2023; ~$350M/yr total advertising.
- **Founder on camera:** John Morgan personally, in nearly every spot.
- **Hook style:** rights education ("you may not know your rights"),
  adversary frame against insurers, occasional cheeky lines ("Size
  matters") aimed at younger viewers.
- **Credibility move:** specific large verdicts named, e.g. $120M against
  the City of Gainesville.
- **Lesson for us:** founder-on-camera + rights-education hook is the
  highest-recall combination in the category.

### Jim Adler ("The Texas Hammer")

- **Iconography:** sledgehammer, highway shot, single phrase ("Go with
  Jim"). Visual brand carried for decades.
- **Practice scale:** 300+ employees built largely on TV.
- **Lesson for us:** one ownable visual + one ownable phrase. We don't
  have to invent a hammer, but the end card needs to be ownable enough
  that someone driving past Buckeye's billboard later closes the loop.

### Cellino & Barnes

- **Iconography:** the jingled phone number (800‑888‑8888). The melody is
  the brand.
- **Lesson for us:** the phone number is the *most-repeated asset.*
  1‑800‑411‑PAIN is already built for this — short, mnemonic, on-brand.
  Hold it on screen, say it twice in 30 seconds, every time.

### Ken Nugent ("One Call, That's All")

- **Lesson:** a single-promise tagline reduces decision friction. "Find
  out what your case is really worth" is in the same DNA — it gives the
  viewer one job.

### Common pattern across all four

1. Adversary hook in first 3 seconds (the insurance company is the bad
   guy).
2. Founder on camera, named, in person.
3. Specific dollar credibility number with bar-compliant disclaimer.
4. One CTA, one phone number, repeated.
5. Free / no fee unless we win.

This is the structure the new spot in `commercials/` is built on.

## On the "average $200K" framing — why we didn't use it

Per the user's instinct ("I don't know"), we checked the actual data.

- **National average car-accident settlement, 2026:** ~$30,400.
- **Minor injury / soft-tissue range:** $15K–$35K.
- **Serious injuries with surgery or long-term care:** $80K–$250K.
- **High-cost states (e.g., NY):** average ~$287K — 9× the national
  average. Ohio sits closer to the national average.

A blanket "average $200K" claim would be inaccurate and triggers Ohio
Rule of Professional Conduct 7.1 (no false or misleading communications).
It also opens up insurance-defense counsel to use the spot against
clients in mediation: "your own lawyer's TV ad says $200K is average, so
your $40K offer is actually generous."

The honest, equally compelling alternative: "**find out what your case
is really worth**" + Buckeye's real and verifiable "**$1B+ recovered for
clients**" claim. Same emotional payload, defensible in any disciplinary
review.

## Geofence audience — what they're feeling, what to show them

Targets the user named — **hospitals, urgent cares, body shops /
collision centers** — plus secondary candidates: tow yards, chiropractors,
PT clinics, auto-glass shops, DMV branches near hospitals.

Mental state when the ad serves:

1. **Disoriented** — adrenaline crash, pain, paperwork dread.
2. **Financially anxious** — "how am I paying for this car / this ER
   bill?"
3. **Skeptical of insurance** — already had a friction call with the
   carrier.
4. **Looking for an adult in the room** — someone who'll handle it for
   them.

The creative should land in that order: *I see you → I know what they're
doing to you → I'll handle it → here's the number*. The new spot is
sequenced exactly to that.

## Geofence ethics & ad-platform rules (worth flagging)

- The Massachusetts AG settled with Copley Advertising in 2017 over
  hospital-perimeter targeting; the practice is legal in most states but
  politically charged. Ohio is not Massachusetts, but the optics carry.
- Some demand-side platforms restrict targeting *inside* healthcare
  facilities for health-related ads. Targeting the **parking lots and
  immediate vicinity** of hospitals (rather than inside) is the lower-
  risk play. Body shops and collision centers don't carry the same
  sensitivity.
- ABA Model Rule 7.3 (solicitation) is fine with broadcast-style ads to
  a general audience; the spot itself is safe. The *targeting layer* is
  what draws scrutiny, not the creative.
- Practical recommendation: keep the creative non-exploitative (no
  graphic injuries, no "we know you're at the hospital right now" copy
  — the spot delivered as written passes this test).

## Open questions to resolve before production

1. Does Greg have an existing voice library / podcast / deposition audio
   we can use for the clone training set, or do we need to schedule a
   recording session? (Cleaner training audio = far better clone.)
2. Is "1‑800‑411‑PAIN" still the lead number, or is the firm pushing a
   short code or vanity web?
3. Bar-compliance review: which Ohio Rule 7.x disclaimers must appear
   on screen ("advertising material," past results disclaimer, principal
   office, responsible attorney)?
4. Do we have 2–3 specific past verdict figures we want to super on
   screen (e.g., a $X.XM truck case, a $X.XM med‑mal case)? Specific
   beats round.
5. Music license budget — needle-drop vs. custom score changes the feel
   a lot, and custom is cheaper across many variants.
